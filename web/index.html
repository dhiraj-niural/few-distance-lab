A small hint

What is the mathematical catch?

Use as few different distances as possible, while keeping this rule: every four points must determine at least three different distances. A square fails because its sides and diagonals give only two lengths.

This board is stretched vertically by √2, so the point labelled (x, y) really sits at (x, √2 y). For two grid points, their squared distance is dx² + 2dy². The numbers stay exact integers; equal squared lengths mean equal lengths.

The stretched lattice is a known construction satisfying the four-point rule. The original Python study also checked every four-point subset of the complete boards used here. Every selection you make inherits that property.

This is a finite playground inspired by Benjamin Grayzel’s work on Erdős problem #659. The lattice construction and the underlying mathematics are prior work. This project does not claim a new theorem or AI-discovery advantage.

What do the targets mean?

8 points, 4 × 4 board: 6 distances is optimal on this board. Exhaustive enumeration checked all 12,870 selections. This says nothing about the optimum over all points in the plane.

10 points, 6 × 6 board: 8 distances is a verified construction. It is a target you can reach, not a proved minimum.

20 points, 7 × 7 board: 17 distances is a verified construction. The saved construction and its equal-score component have no improving one- or two-point replacement. That is a local result. Whether this board admits 16 or fewer remains unproved in this project.

Revealing a construction loads the actual saved points. You can change them, inspect repeated lengths, or export your own witness for the independent Python checkers.

Enter coordinates or verify an export

An export is a certificate compatible with the repository’s exact verifier. Save the downloaded file and run:

python3 -m few_distance verify witness.json

At least four points are required for export. No account, server, analytics or external computation is used. Board links store the coordinates in the URL.
